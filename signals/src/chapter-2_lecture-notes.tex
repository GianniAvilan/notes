\section{Chapter 2: Time-Domain Analysis of Continuous Time Systems} 
\setcounter{subsection}{0}
\subsection{Introduction}

To begin, we'll revisit the properties of a linear system that we covered briefly in Chapter 1. If you recall, a linear system meets two mathematical criteria: \textbf{additivity} and \textbf{homogeneity}. In practice, this means that any linear combination of individual inputs to such a system will result in an equivalent, linear combination of the corresponding outputs. Together, the two properties are referred to as \textbf{superposition}, and may be expressed mathematically by saying that if \(x_1 \to y_1\) and \(x_2 \to y_2\), then for all inputs \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) and all constants \(k_1\) and \(k_2\), 
\[
  k_1x_1 + k_2x_2 \to k_1y_1 + k_2y_2.
\]

Thinking for a moment about the type of physical systems we're likely to encounter in the practice of engineering, such as complex electrical networks with multiple storage elements, their response (or output) to some input signal \(x(t)\) at \(t \geq 0\) (that is, for a causal system) is determined by two factors: the initial state of the system at \(t=0\) and its response to the input signal \(x(t)\), neglecting the initial state. Going forward, these two separate responses will be referred to as the system's zero-input response (ZIR) and its zero-state response (ZSR). Why is this detail important? Since we'll be dealing exclusively with linear, time-invariant, continous (LTIC) systems in this chapter, we can view the zero input condition and the zero state condition as two separate inputs to the system, \(x_1(t)\) and \(x_2(t)\), and their corresponding outputs as \(y_1(t)\) and \(y_2(t)\). Going back our earlier definition of a linear system, this means that if we can determine the system's individual responses to each condition, in other words, its ZIR and its ZSR, the sum of those two response will accurately represent the system's \textit{total response} to the sum of both inputs. 

Put simply, total response = ZIR + ZSR.

This correlate of the superposition property is known as the \textbf{decomposition property}. We'll come back to a few other natural and extremely useful consquences of superposition and decomposition momentarily, but first, we'll dive into a brief review of linear ordinary differential equations, their solutions, and their relationship to systems. 

Though systems and their corresponding equations can become incredibly complex, there is a large class of systems whose response to inputs can be expressed using a linear, ordinary differential equation. First of all, what is a differential equation? In short, a differential equation defines some mathematical, input output relationship \(y(t) = x(t)\) where the input and output's rates of change (derivatives, mathematically) depend on their current state. Many systems that arise from physics naturally have these sorts of dynamics. For example, a simple RC circuit, which involves a capacitor and a resistor placed in series with a voltage source, can be modeled in terms the input voltage at the source \(v_s(t)\) and the "output" voltage across the capacitor \(v_c(t)\) as 
\[
  v_s = RC \frac{dv_c(t)}{dt} + v_c(t).
\]

The solution to this differential equation, \(v_c(t)\), represents the system's response to an input voltage \(v_s(t)\), and depends on the resistance, capacitance, and initial conditions (how charged the capacitor is) at \(t=0\).

In the most general sense, the LTIC systems that we will deal with in this chapter may be defined by a linear differential equation of the form 
\begin{align*}
	&\frac{d^Ny(t)}{dt^N} + a_1 \frac{d^{N-1}y(t)}{dt^{N-1}} + \ ... \ + a_{N-1}\frac{dy(t)}{dt} + a_Ny(t) = \\
	&b_{N-M}\frac{d^Mx(t)}{dt^M} + b_{N-M+1}\frac{d^{M-1}x(t)}{dt^{M-1}} + \ .. \ + b_{N-1}\frac{dx(t)}{dt}+b_Nx(t).
\end{align*} 

In other words, LTIC systems are often described in terms of N-th and M-th order differential equations, with \(M \leq N\) in most cases. \(N\) simply refers to the highest-order derivative on the left-hand side, and \(M\) to the highest-order derivative on the right-hand side. A \textit{linear} differential equation is simply one in which each derivative on either side is multiplied by a unique, constant coefficient.

To make notation easier, the above can be simplified by using \(\frac{d^N}{dt^N} = D^N\), which then reduces the general case to
\begin{align*}
	&(D^N + a_1D^{N-1} + \ ... \ + a_{N-1}D + a_N)y(t) = \\
	&(b_{N-M}D^M + b_{N-M+1}D^{M-1} + \ ... \ b_{N-1}D+b_N)x(t).
\end{align*}

Further reducing \(D^N + a-1D^{N-1} + \ ... \ + a_{N-1} + a_N\) to \(Q(D)\) and the corresponding coefficient for the right side as \(P(D)\), the most compact representation of an LTIC system in this form is \[
  Q(D)y(t) = P(D)x(t).
\]

Note that this is not the same as the description of the system that we dealt with previously, which we represented as \(y(t) = x(t)\). Given a linear differential equation like the one shown above, its solution \(y(t)\), along with any relevant initial conditions, will correspond to the more familiar form of \(y(t) = x(t).\) The goal of any analysis, when given the differential equation describing an LTIC system, is to find that system's response to a specific input; in other words, solve the differential equation to find \(y(t)\) for a given \(x(t)\).

From here forward, to keep notation compact, we will use the dot notation standard for derivatives; that is, the first derivative of \(y(t)\), usually represented as \(\frac{dy(t)}{dt}\) or \(y'(t)\), is represented as \(\dot{y}(t)\), its second derivative as \(\ddot{y}(t)\), and so on.

Further analysis of LTIC systems can be made easier by first deriving the general solution to a linear differential equation of the form \(Q(D)y(t) = 0\) (the reason why will become clear in the next section). 

To demonstrate a generalized solution for \(y(t)\) in this case, we will test \(y(t) = e^{\lambda t}\) as a possible solution to the equation. Recall that derivatives of \(e^{\lambda t}\) follow a repeating pattern, that is, \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda e^{\lambda t}\), \(\frac{d^2}{dt^2}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda^2 e^{\lambda t}\), and so on. Substiuting \(y(t) = e^{\lambda t}\) into \(Q(D)y(t) = 0\),
  \[
		\lambda^N e^{\lambda t} + a_1 \lambda ^{N-1} e^{\lambda t} + \ ... \  + a_{N-1} \lambda e^{\lambda t} + a_N e^{\lambda t} = 0.
  \]
	Factoring \(e^{\lambda t}\) out of the equation, we are left with \[
		e^{\lambda t}(\lambda ^N + a_1 \lambda^{N-1} + \ ... \ a_{N-1}\lambda + a_N) = 0.
	\]
	Since \(e^{\lambda t}\), for any value of \(\lambda\), never equals zero, the logical conclusion is that for the above equation to balance, \(\lambda^N + a_1\lambda^{N-1} + \ ... \ a_{N-1}\lambda + a_N = 0\). Recall that for any real differential equation that we will be dealing with here as the description of a system, the coefficents \(a_1\) to \(a_N\) will be given to you. With that in mind, the previous equation becomes a simple, \(N\)th order polynomial. Finding its roots \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2, \ ... \ , \lambda_N\), we will have a solution that balances or original equation. This polynomial is often referred to as \(Q(\lambda)\), or the \textbf{characteristic equation}. When given a real differential equation, using the notation given before, it will often be provided in the form \((D^N + a_2D^{N-1} + \ ... \ + a_{N-1}D + a_N)y(t) = 0\). We can then easily solve the characteristic equation by replacing each \(D\) with \(\lambda\) and finding the roots of the resulting polynomial.

	(There are more interesting ways to prove this, and that this is the only valid solution for a linear differential equation of this form, using power series, but for now it's easiest to just substitute \(y(t) = e^{\lambda t}\) and show that it matches with the constraints imposed by \(Q(D)y(t) = 0\).)

	Once we have our \(N\) values of \(\lambda\), we can replace it in our original equation for \(y(t)\). Since all values of \(\lambda\) are valid solutions, the full solution for \(y(t)\) is given by a linear combination of what we will call the system's \textbf{characteristic modes}, as 
	\[
		y(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + c_2e^{\lambda_2 t} + \ ... \ + c_Ne^{\lambda_N t}.
	\] 

	The remaining constant terms \(c_1\) through \(c_N\) can be solved using initial conditions. For an \(N\)th order equation, \(N\) initial conditions will be needed, \(y(0)\) through \(\frac{d^Ny(0)}{dt^N}\). Assuming \(t=0\), \(e^0 = 1\), so we can then solve for our constants as 
	\begin{align*}
		& c_1 + c_2 + \ ... \ c_N = y(0) \\
		& \lambda_1c_1 + \lambda_2c_2 + \ ... \ + \lambda_Nc_N = \dot{y}(0),
	\end{align*}
	and so on.

\setcounter{subsection}{1}
\subsection{Zero-Input Response}

If we recall our earlier discussion on linearity, any LTIC system's total response to a causal signal \(x(t)\) can be solved for by finding its ZIR and ZSR separately, and then summing the two responses together. 

We start by finding the system's ZIR, \(y_0(t)\). By reducing the input to \(x(t) = 0\), the system's associated linear differential equation reduces to \[
  Q(D)y_0(t) = 0,
\] 
and solving for \(y_0(t)\) will produce the ZIR that we're looking for. 

Thankfully, if we simply use the process outlined above, we can easily find the system's ZIR, the first piece of the puzzle.

	Summarizing, the following are the general steps for finding the ZIR of any LTIC system described by a linear differential equation of the form \(Q(D)y(t) = P(D)x(t)\):

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Set \(x(t) = 0\), which leaves \(Q(D)y(t) = 0\).
		\item Given \(Q(D) = (D^N + a_1D^{N-1} + \ ... \ + a_{N-1}D + a_N)\), find the system's characteristic roots, \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ ... \ , \lambda_N,\) by solving \(Q(D) = 0\).
		\item Express \(y_0(t)\) as a linear combination of the system's characteristic modes, so that \(y_0(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1} + c_2e^{\lambda_2} + \ ... \ + c_Ne^{\lambda_N}.\)
		\item Using \(N\) initial conditions, solve for constants \(c_1, c_2, \ ... \ , c_N\).
	\end{enumerate}

	As a final note, it's possible that the solution of the characteristic equation of a system returns repeated roots, or complex roots. If this is the case, the form of the solution \(y_0(t)\) will be slightly different. The textbook references both these cases in detail.

\setcounter{subsection}{2}
\subsection{The Unit Impulse Response \(\mathbf{h(t)}\)}

Having found a simple way to solve for the ZIR of an LTIC system, we may now move on to the second half of the total response: the ZSR. As mentioned in the introduction, the \(ZSR\) refers to the system's output when the given signal \(x(t)\) is applied, \textit{ignoring} the system's initial state, since that information is already solved for in the ZIR.

The ZSR is slightly more complicated to solve for than the ZIR, but may also be reduced to a simple formula. In Chapter 1, the Dirac delta function \(\delta(t)\) was defined in terms of its effect on other functions; we'll take full advantage of that so-called sampling property in finding an LTIC system's ZSR. Recall that the sampling property says that, given some function (in this case, our input signal \(x(t)\)), \[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\delta(t\pm\tau)dt = x(\tau).
\] 
In other words, when the product of the signal and the Dirac delta function are integrated along the entire range of the function, the output consists solely of \(x(t)\)'s value at \(\tau\). Using this property, we can model an input signal \(x(t)\) as a train of infintesimal unit impulse functions, which, when integrated, combine to form the complete signal \(x(t).\)
 
Therefore, simply by knowing the mathematical description of the input signal \(x(t)\) and the systems response to the unit impulse \(\delta(t)\), we can solve for the system's response to \(x(t)\) in its totality. We'll return to this idea of combining a train of unit impulse functions, but first we'll solve for the system's response to a single unit impulse \(\delta(t)\). We will refer to this as the system's \textbf{impulse response}, \(h(t).\)

So how can we find \(h(t)\)? First, assuming a single unit impulse as the input to the system, we can then adjust our system \(Q(D)y(t) = P(D)y(t)\) to match these new constraints, as \[
	Q(D)h(t) = P(D)\delta(t).
\]
Recall that \(\delta(t)\) itself is only defined at \(t=0\), and so we may consider the effect of \(\delta(t)\) as a kind of initial condition that is provided to \(y(t)\). As we saw in the section on ZIR, an LTIC system's response to no input consists of a linear combination of its characteristic modes, \(c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + \ ... \ c_Ne^{\lambda_N t}\). Since the input \(x(t) = \delta(t) = 0\) for \(t > 0\), we can assume that the system's response for \(t>0\) is the same as its response to zero input; that is, its characteristic modes. Returning once again to the superposition property, this means that we can express the systems ZSR as a combination of this zero-input response and its response to the unit impulse at \(t=0\).

We'll use some concrete examples to explain behavior at \(t=0\), starting with the simplest one. Beginning with \(h(t) = \delta(t)\), where there are no system dynamics, the answer is trivial: \(h(t)\) must itself be the unit impulse function, \(\delta(t)\), to meet our criteria.

Adding dynamics to the system, but keeping \(M=N\), \((D+1)h(t) = (D+1)\delta(t)\) is the next logical step. This linear differential equation essentially asks, what function, when summed with its derivative, is equal to the unit impulse function plus \textit{its} derivative? Again the answer is trivial: \(h(t) = \delta(t)\). We may reasonably conclude that for any system of this sort, \(h(t) = A_0\delta(t)\), where \(A_0\) is some constant determined by the coefficients of \(Q(D)\) and \(P(D)\). Thus, when \(M=N\), \(h(t) = A_0\delta(t) + (c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + \ ... \ + c_Ne^{\lambda_N t})u(t)\) (since the characteristic modes occur only for \(t>0\)).

What about when \(M < N\), such as in the case \((D+1)h(t) = \delta(t)\)? Here, we're looking for some function \(h(t)\) that, when summed with its first derivative, results in a single delta function at \(t=0\). Like with our initial discussion on differential equations, we'll test a function we know to be the correct answer, confirm it's accurate, and avoid the details of its derivation for the time being. Testing \(e^{-t}u(t)\), \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t}u(t) = -e^{-t}u(t) + e^{-t}\delta(t)\) (use the product rule if you want to derive this yourself), and so \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t}u(t) + e^{-t}u(t) = e^{-t}u(t) - e^{-t}u(t) + e^{-t}\delta(t)\), which reduces to \(h(t) = e^{-t}\delta(t)\). Again, at \(t=0\), \(e^0=1\), so \(h(0) = \delta(t)\). Therefore, for a system where \(M < N\), the impulse response \(h(t)\) may be given, more generally, as \(h(t) = (c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + \ ... \ + c_Ne^{\lambda t})u(t)\).   

Lastly, we will need to solve for \(c_1, \ ... \ , c_N\) with the use of initial conditions before defining our completed ZSR. How can we solve for those? The given or found initial conditions of the system were already solved for in the ZIR, but we can only assume that the application of a unit impulse to the system will result in new initial conditions.

Assuming the signal is causal, we know that \(h(t)\) and all its derivatives are zero prior to \(t=0\), but what about at at \(t=0\)? Going back to our assumption that \(\delta(t)\) sets new initial conditions on the system, we can assign placeholder values to those new initial conditions \(K_1, \ ... \ , K_N\), so that \(h(0^+) = K_1\), \(\dot{h}(0^+) = K_2\), and so on. The only function that can take us from \(0\) to \(K_1\) or \(K_2\) in such a short time is \(u(t)\). Considering we've found the response of the system for \(t>0\), we want to focus solely on what's happening at \(t=0\). To keep our notation manageable here, we'll refer to the systems response for \(t>0\) as \(h_1(t)\). Then we can say that \(h(t) = K_1u(t) + h_1(t)\) and \(\dot{h}(t) = K_2u(t) + \dot{h}_1(t)\).

Taking the derivative of both these functions, 
\begin{align*}
	& \dot{h}(t) = K_1\delta(t) + \dot{h}_1(t) \\
	& \ddot{h}(t) = K_1\dot{\delta}(t) + K_2\delta(t) + \ddot{h}_1(t).
\end{align*}

Neglecting the systems response for \(t>0\), we now have 
\begin{align*}
	&\dot{h}(0) = K_1\delta(t) \\
	&\ddot{h}(0) = K_1\dot{\delta}(t) + K_2\delta(t),
\end{align*}
and so on for any number of derivatives.

How can we then use these to find our new initial conditions \(K_1\) through \(K_N\)? We may use a technique known as impulse matching to help us find their values. 

I think the textbook's Example 2.5 misses some important details, so I'll walk through it step by step, and hopefully that will clarify the impulse matching technique.

The given equation describing the system in Example 2.5 is \((D^2 + 5D + 6)y(t) = (D+1)x(t)\). Since we're looking for the impulse response \(h(t)\), we'll set \(y(t) = h(t)\) and \(x(t) = \delta(t)\). That then reduces to \[
	\ddot{h}(t) + 5\dot{h}(t) + 6h(t) = \dot{\delta}(t) + \delta(t).
\]

Without going through the entire derivation like we did before, we can move faster by assuming that since \(M=1< N=2\), there will be no \(\delta(t)\) term in the impulse response \(h(t)\). Since the left-hand side is a second-order derivative, we expect a response of the form \[
	h(t) = (c_1e^{\lambda_1} + c_2e^{\lambda_2})u(t).
\]

Our first step is to solve the characteristic equation, derived from the right-hand side of the differential equation, as \[
	(D^2 + 5D + 6) = \lambda^2 + 5\lambda + 6 = 0.
\]
 
Solving for \(\lambda\), \begin{align*}
  &\lambda_1 = -2 \\
	&\lambda_2 = -3.
\end{align*}

Substituting \(\lambda\) back into our \(h(t)\), \[
	h(t) = (c_1e^{-2t} + c_2e^{-3t})u(t).
\]

Now, we'll use impulse matching to find the two initial conditions we need, \({h}(0)\) and \(\dot{h}(0)\), or \(K_1\) and \(K_2\). Recall that we found earlier that \(\dot{h}(t) = \delta(t)K_1\) and \(\ddot{h}(t) = \dot{\delta}(t)K_1 + \delta(t)K_2\).

Plugging those into our given differential equation to substitute for \(\ddot{h}(t)\) and \(\dot{h}(t)\) at \(t=0\):
\begin{align}
	&\ddot{h}(0) + 5\dot{h}(0) + 6h(0) = \ddot{\delta}(t) + \delta(t)\\
	&\dot{\delta}(t)K_1 + \delta(t)K_2 + 5\delta(t)K_1 + h(0) = \dot{\delta}(t) + \delta(t).
\end{align}

Then, we can collect coefficents of \(\dot{\delta}(t)\) and \(\delta(t)\) on the left-hand side, 
\[
	\dot{\delta(t)}K_1 + \delta(t)(5K_1 + K_2) + h(0) = \dot{\delta}(t) + \delta(t).
\]

This example is pretty simple: on the right-hand side, \(\dot{\delta}(t)\) and \(\delta(t)\) both have coefficients of \(1\).Matching the coefficients of those two impulse terms on the right-hand side, we get
\begin{align*}
	& K_1 = 1 \\
	& 5K_1 + K_2 = 1,
\end{align*}

which solves as 
\begin{align*}
	&K_1 = 1 \\
	&K_2 = -4
\end{align*}

Now, we can find \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) by setting initial conditions equal to our new \(K_1\) and \(K_2\), as 
\begin{align*}
	&h(0) = c_1 + c_2 = K_1 = 1 \\
	&\dot{h}(0) = -2c_1 - 3c_2 = K_2 = -4,
\end{align*}

which yields 
\begin{align*}
	&c_1 = -1\\
	&c_2 = 2
\end{align*}

Substituting this into our original equation for \(h(t)\),
\[
	h(t) = (-e^{-2t} + 2e^{-3t})u(t).
\]

Finally, this is the system's impulse response \(h(t)\).

As promised, there is also a formulaic way to find \(h(t)\), and I'll briefly review it here. 

\begin{enumerate}
	\item First, given the form \(Q(D)y(t) = P(D)x(t)\), assume \(h(t) = b_0\delta(t) + [P(D)y_n(t)]u(t)\). Since we are given \(P(D)\), we now just need to find \(b_0\) and \(y_n(t)\)
	\item \(y_n(t)\) here stands for the familiar linear combination of the system's characteristic modes, \(c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + \ ... \ + c_Ne^{\lambda_N t}\). Rather than painstakingly solving for initial conditions as before, though, we can simply assume that \(y_n^{N-1}(0) = 1\), and all other initial conditions are 0.
	\item Finally, \(b_0\) will simply be the coefficient of the \(N\)th derivative of \(x(t)\) on the right hand side. If \(M < N\), \(b_0 = 0\), and so no \(\delta(t)\) term is needed.
	\item Putting the above pieces together, we have solved for the system's impulse response \(h(t)\).
\end{enumerate}

\setcounter{subsection}{3}
\subsection{System Response to External Input: The Zero-State Response}

The impulse response \(h(t)\) leads directly to the ZSR of the system, by using the aforementioned approach of modeling the input signal \(x(t)\) as a train of impulses. Once again revisiting linearity, the sum of the system's impulse response to that train will define its overall response to the input.

Without laboring over the math (which you can find in the textbook), the most intuitive way to visualize the process is to reduce the value of \(x(t)\) at each instant \(\tau\) to a unit impulse function whose width approaches \(0\) and whose height approaches \(\infty\), multiplied by the value of \(x(\tau)\) at that point. Summing the impulse response to each of these terms will provide the system's total response to \(x(t)\) neglecting initial conditions covered in the ZIR, and will, as such, provide the systems ZSR.

Mathematically, this ZSR looks like 
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\tau.
\]

Thankfully, we have a tool built for this kind of problem: convolution. If you'll recall from previous classes convolution is defined as 
\[
  x_1(t) * x_2(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x_1(\tau)x_2(t-\tau)d\tau,
\]
exactly the type of process we're looking for.

Rather than rehashing graphical convolution along with the mathematical reasoning behind the process, it suffices to say that the ZSR can now readily be found by means of a convolution table and/or a transformation to the frequency domain.

Now, we are finally ready to find our system's total response, and the answer is as simple as \[
	y(t) = \mathrm{ZIR} + \mathrm{ZSR}.
\]
Expanding this further,
\[
	y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}c_ke^{\lambda_kt} + x(t) * h(t).
\]
Put simply, the total response is the sum of the linear combination of the system's characteristic modes (ZIR) and the convolution of the input signal with the system's impulse response (ZSR).

\subsection{System Stability}

We'll now briefly return to stability considerations that were first discussed in Chapter 1. 

Rather than guessing at a system's stability using its description, it's simpler to begin any discussion of a system's overall stability based on its total response to any input \(x(t)\). 

We'll begin with bounded-input/bounded-output (BIBO) stability, which simply states that, for some system, as long as its input is bounded (not infinite), its output will remain bouned (not infinite).

How does total repsonse factor into this? Equilibrium states are states in which a system can stay forever, and are related to its characteristic modes. We may further subdivide equilibrium states into several sub-categories: stable equilibrium refers to a system that can return to equilibrium after small disturbances, unstable equilibrium to a system that will move further and further from its equlibrium point in response to small disturbances, and netural equilibrium to a system that does neither in response to a small disturbance.

Proving a system is BIBO stable can be accomplished by integrating its impulse response and defining the output, 
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|h(\tau)|d\tau < \infty.
\]

(Review the proof for this later).

Proving a system is BIBO \textit{unstable} would require just a single counterexample, or a mathematical proof. Proving \textit{stability}, on the other hand, requires a proof, since it must be proved for all possible inputs \(x(t)\).   

Moving on from BIBO stability, asymptotic (internal) stability defines a system whose output returns to zero over time if given no external inputs, and is defined by the behavior of the characteristic modes; e.g., a complex mode of the form \(e^{jt} = \cos{(t)} + j\sin{(t)}\), and oscillates forever.

Characteristic roots \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ ... \ \lambda_n\) can be complex, e.g., \(\lambda_n = \sigma + j\omega\). Defining all roots as complex, we can then define the system using the properties of \(\sigma\). If \(\sigma < 0\), the system will decay to zero; \(\sigma > 0\), the system will grow indefinitely; and \(\sigma = 0\), the system will neither grow nor decay. These properties can be quickly deduced using the \(\sigma-\omega\) plane. In the left half-plane, we find stability; in the right, instability.

In short, judgements on asymptotic stability can be made most quickly and efficiently based on \textit{just} the roots \(\lambda\) of the system. IMPORTANT: internal stability implies BIBO stability. If the system is not asymptotically stable, it is BIBO unstable as well.

If the characteristic roots are not provided (e.g., just \(h(t)\) is known) then the impulse response can be used to assess BIBO stability. Methods of finding the stability depend on the information provided.
