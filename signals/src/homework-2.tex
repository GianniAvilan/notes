	\item Given the system described by 
		\[
		\ddot{y}(t) + 2\dot{y}(t) + 5y(t) = \ddot{x}(t) - 5x(t),
		\]
		with initial conditions
		\begin{align*}
		 &y_{\mathrm{zir}}(0) = 4 \\
		 &\dot{y}_{\mathrm{zir}}(0) = -1,
		\end{align*}
		begin by setting \(P(D)x(t)\) to zero to find the zero-input response, defined as \(y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + c_2e^{\lambda_2 t}.\)

		To find \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), we will solve the characteristic equation \(\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5=0 \), which produces two complex roots, 
		\[
			\lambda_1 = -1+j2, \ \lambda_2 = -1-j2.
		\]
		We can pick one of two ways to solve for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\): we can keep the ZIR in complex form and solve for the \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), or we can solve for the constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) more easily by converting the ZIR to a real form. In that case, \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) must be conjugates, expressed as 
		\begin{align*}
			& c_1 = \frac{c}{2}e^{j\theta} \\
			& c_2 = \frac{c}{2}e^{-j\theta}.
		\end{align*}
		Substituting these new constants into our expression for \(y_{\mathrm{zir}}\), 
		\[
			y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = \frac{c}{2}e^{j\theta}e^{(-1+j2)t} + \frac{c}{2}e^{-j\theta}e^{(-1-j2)t} = \frac{c}{2}[e^{-t}e^{j(2t+\theta)} + e^{-t}e^{j(-2t-\theta)}]. 
		\]
		Simplifying using Euler's formula, \(e^{jx} = \cos{(x)} + j\sin{(x)}\),
		\[
			y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = \frac{ce^{-t}}{2}[\cos{(2t+\theta)} + j\sin{(2t+\theta)} + \cos{(-2t-\theta)} + j\sin{(-2t-\theta)}.]
		\]
		Since \(\cos{(-x)} = \cos{(x)}\) and \(\sin{(-x)} = -\sin{(x)}\), we may further reduce the ZIR to 
		\[
		\frac{ce^{-t}}{2}(2\cos{(2t+\theta)}) = ce^{-t}\cos{(2t+\theta)}.
		\]
		Now, we may easily solve for \(c\) and \(\theta\) as real values by setting \(t=0\) for \(y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t)\) and \(\dot{y}_{\mathrm{zir}}(t)\), 
		\begin{align*}
		 & y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = ce^{-t}\cos{(2t+\theta)} \\
		 & \dot{y}_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = -ce^{-t}\cos{(2t+\theta)} - 2ce^{-t}\sin{(2t+\theta)}.
		\end{align*}
		Setting these expressions equal to the given initial conditions at \(t=0\), 
		\begin{align*}
			&y_{\mathrm{zir}}(0) = c\cos{(\theta)} = 4 \\ 
			&\dot{y}_{\mathrm{zir}}(0) = -c\cos{(\theta)} - 2c\sin{(\theta)} = -1.
		\end{align*}
		Solving for \(c\cos{(\theta)}\) and \(c\sin{(\theta)}\) using a system of equations,  
		\begin{align*}
			&\begin{bmatrix}
				1 & 0 \\
        -1	& -2
			\end{bmatrix}
			\begin{bmatrix}
				c\cos{(\theta)} \\
				c\sin{(\theta)}
			\end{bmatrix} = 
			\begin{bmatrix}
				4 \\
				-1
			\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
			&\begin{bmatrix}
				1 & 0 & 4 \\
				-1 & -2 & -1
			\end{bmatrix}
			\Rightarrow \ 
			\begin{matrix}
				&c\cos{(\theta)} = 4 \\
				&c\sin{(\theta)} = -1.5
			\end{matrix}
		\end{align*}
		Considering both terms to be the coordinates of a complex number mapped onto the x-y plane, we may solve for \(c\) and \(\theta\) as \(c = \sqrt{4^2 + 1.5^2} = 4.272\) and \(\theta = \tan^{-1}{\frac{-1.5}{4}} = -0.359\), we can substitute this back into our solution for \(y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t)\), which gives us the complete ZIR, 
		\[
			y_{\mathrm{zir}}(t) = 4.272e^{-t}\cos{(2t - 0.359)}.
		\]
		Finally, we will convert our values for \(c\) and \(\theta\) back to \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) if we want, as 
		\begin{align*}
			&c_1 = \frac{c}{2}e^{j\theta} = 2.136e^{-j0.359} \\
			&c_2 = \frac{c}{2}e^{-j\theta} = 2.136e^{j0.359}.
		\end{align*}
		The result is a decaying sinusoid, which is to be expected given complex-valued characteristic roots.

					BIBO stability is also known as \textit{external} stability, and is related to the stability of the system when provided with some external input. Given a bounded signal (ones that does not grow towards infinity over time), a BIBO-stable system provides a bounded output (one that does not grow towards infinity over time). As one might expect, BIBO stability can be determined from the system's impulse response \(h(t)\), where BIBO-stable systems meet the criteria 
					\[
					  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|h(\tau)|d\tau < \infty
					\]

					BIBO stability is a natural consequence of asymptotic or \textit{internal} stability, which, as the name suggests, depends on the system's internal dynamics when presented with no input signal. A system's asymptotic stability can be determined based on its characteristic roots; if any lie in the right half of the s-plane, the system is asymptotically unstable.
					\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
						\item 
							\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
								\item To determine BIBO stability, we'll investigate the impulse response \(h(t)\) of the system described by \((D^2 + 8D + 12)y(t) = (D-1)x(t)\). Using the simplified impulse matching method shown before and solving for \(h(t)\) in MATLAB, 
									\[
										h(t) = (\frac{e^{-6t} + 3e^{-2t}}{4})u(t).
									\]
									Then, evaluating the absolute integral of \(h(t)\),
									\[
									  \int_{0}^{\infty}|h(\tau)|d\tau \approx 0.244,
									\]
									and so the system is \textbf{BIBO-stable}.

								\item BIBO-stability on its own does not imply asymptotic stability, so we'll check the criteria for asymptotic stability as well.
									
									Solving the system's characteristic equation \(\lambda^2 + 8\lambda + 12 = 0\), we will find characteristic modes 
								\begin{align*}
									&\lambda_1 = -6 \\
									&\lambda_2 = -2
								\end{align*}	
								Since both resultant characteristic modes, \(e^{-6t}\) and \(e^{-2t}\), have a negative real part, and are thus in the left side of the s-plane, the system is considered \textbf{asymptotically stable}.
							\end{enumerate}
